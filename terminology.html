02 / THE FOUNDATIONS

Words build
understanding.

A plain-language glossary for a more thoughtful conversation. Explore the terms, then see what they can mean in everyday life.

18 terms to explore

Foundations

Neurodiversity

The natural variation in how human minds work. The term describes diversity across people, rather than a diagnosis.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A class includes many ways of thinking, learning, and communicating.

Foundations

Neurodivergent

A broad, non-medical term for a person whose mental functioning differs from what is considered typical. People and communities use its boundaries differently.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Someone with ADHD may describe themself as neurodivergent.

Foundations

Neurotypical

A term for someone whose neurological development or functioning is considered typical in a particular context. It does not mean everyone in that group thinks alike.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A neurotypical person still has individual learning and communication preferences.

Foundations

Neurodiverse

Describes a group of people with different neurological characteristics. An individual is usually described as neurodivergent or neurotypical.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A team with both neurotypical and neurodivergent members is neurodiverse.

Foundations

Disability

A condition or difference that can limit activities or participation. Both individual needs and barriers in the environment affect someone’s experience of disability.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A noisy testing room may create a barrier that a quiet space helps reduce.

Language

Identity-first language

Language that places a disability or identity before the word “person.” Some people see this as recognizing an important part of who they are.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

“Autistic person” is identity-first language. Follow the individual’s preference.

Language

Person-first language

Language that names the person before their disability. Some people prefer it as a way to emphasize their personhood.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

“Person with dyslexia” is person-first language. There is no single preference shared by everyone.

Foundations

Neurodevelopmental conditions

Conditions that arise during development and can affect learning, communication, attention, or movement. Experiences and support needs vary widely.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

ADHD and autism are examples of neurodevelopmental conditions.

Everyday experiences

Hyperfocus

An intense period of attention on an activity, sometimes with difficulty switching attention to something else.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Someone may become so absorbed in a project that they lose track of time.

Everyday experiences

Sensory sensitivity

Experiencing sensory input, such as sound, light, textures, or smells, differently or more intensely. Sensitivity can vary by sense and situation.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A buzzing fluorescent light may make it difficult to concentrate.

Everyday experiences

Special interests

Deep or sustained interests in particular subjects. Many autistic people use this term for interests that bring enjoyment, knowledge, and connection.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A person might enjoy learning extensively about transit maps or a favorite author.

Everyday experiences

Stimming

Repetitive movements, sounds, or actions that can help a person regulate sensory input, express emotion, or focus.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Rocking, humming, or moving the hands may be forms of stimming.

Everyday experiences

Masking / camouflaging

Hiding or changing traits to fit social expectations. This can take effort and may leave a person feeling exhausted.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Someone may rehearse conversations or force eye contact even when it is uncomfortable.

Everyday experiences

Restricted and repetitive behaviors

A broad clinical term that can include repeated movements, routines, and focused interests. Their purpose and impact differ between people.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A familiar routine can help someone feel prepared for the day.

Language

Visible and non-visible disabilities

Some differences are readily apparent; others are not. What you can observe does not reliably tell you how much support someone needs.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A person may experience sensory overload without showing obvious signs of distress.

Support

Accommodations

Changes to an environment or process that help a person participate and access the same opportunities.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Written instructions, quieter spaces, or accessible learning materials may help.

Support

Executive function

Mental processes used to plan, start tasks, organize information, manage time, and shift attention.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A visual checklist can make a multi-step assignment easier to manage.

Everyday experiences

Sensory overload

Feeling overwhelmed when sensory input becomes difficult to process or manage.

IN EVERYDAY LIFE

A crowded, noisy hallway may feel overwhelming; a quieter route can help.

No matching terms

Try a shorter word or choose a different category.

A small distinction, a useful one.

A group can be neurodiverse. An individual may be neurodivergent. Respect how people choose to describe themselves.
